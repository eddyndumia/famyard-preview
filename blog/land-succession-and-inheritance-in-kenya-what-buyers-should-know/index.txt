1f:Tdf2,<p>A buyer once purchased land from a man who described himself, accurately, as “the eldest son.” What he didn’t mention was that three other siblings had never signed off on the sale, and the estate’s succession process had never been formally completed. Two years later, one of those siblings filed a claim. The buyer’s title, on paper, looked fine. In practice, it was built on sand.</p>
<h2>How Succession Works in Kenya</h2>
<p>Under the Law of Succession Act, a deceased person’s land is distributed either according to a valid will (testate succession) or, in its absence, according to statutory rules that divide the estate among the surviving spouse, children, and sometimes other dependents (intestate succession). The process typically requires a grant of representation from the court before the land can be legally transferred to heirs.</p>
<h2>Why Inherited Land Is Higher Risk</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Incomplete succession.</strong> Land is sometimes sold by an heir before the succession process is legally finalized, meaning the seller may not yet hold clear, transferable title.</li>
<li><strong>Unlisted beneficiaries.</strong> If not all rightful heirs were included in the succession process, they may later contest the distribution — and any sale that followed it.</li>
<li><strong>Family disputes.</strong> Succession is a common source of family conflict, and land sold amid an unresolved dispute can leave a buyer caught in the middle.</li>
</ul>
<h2>A Worked Example</h2>
<p>Two siblings inherit a parcel jointly. One wants to sell immediately; the other wants to hold. If the first sibling sells “their half” without the second’s agreement or a completed, court-confirmed distribution, the buyer’s position is far weaker than it appears — because legally, an undivided joint interest isn’t the same as owning a specific, saleable half.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Confirm a grant of representation has been issued and confirmed by the court — not just applied for. “We’re in the process” is not the same as “this is done,” and the gap between the two is where most inherited-land disputes originate.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>What to Verify Before Buying</h2>
<ol>
<li>Confirm a grant of representation has been issued and confirmed by the court</li>
<li>Check that the title has been transferred into the seller’s name (or the estate’s) through the registry, not just informally agreed among family</li>
<li>Ask whether other heirs have consented to the sale, particularly where land is jointly inherited</li>
</ol>
<p>This is another area where <a href="/famyard-preview/blog/legal-assistance-while-buying-land-in-kenya">professional legal input</a> pays for itself, alongside the standard <a href="/famyard-preview/blog/title-deed-verification-process-in-kenya">title verification process</a>.</p>
<h2>FAQ</h2>
<h3>How can I tell if succession has actually been completed?</h3>
<p>Request the confirmed grant of representation and check that title has been formally transferred at the registry — a verbal claim from the seller isn’t sufficient evidence.</p>
<h3>What if I’ve already bought land and later discover succession wasn’t complete?</h3>
<p>Get legal advice immediately — resolution options exist, but they’re far more limited after payment than before it, which is exactly why this check belongs before you pay.</p>
<h2>Bottom Line</h2>
<p>Inherited land isn’t inherently riskier than any other land — but it requires confirming that succession was actually completed, not just claimed, before you pay a shilling.</p>
